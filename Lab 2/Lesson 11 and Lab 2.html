Lecture 11: signals on the sphere

By the end of the lecture you should be able to: explain why the sphere has no regular grid that can be refined; expand a sky map in spherical harmonics and read its power spectrum \(C_\ell\) with cosmic variance; explain why going from coefficients to a map is exact but the way back is not; describe HEALPix and its fast ring transform; pick \(N_{\rm side}\) from the beam; and recover the sky spectrum from a pixelised, beam-smoothed map.

A0

Lectures 7 to 11 form one story: take a continuous signal, turn it into a list of numbers, and learn what you can compute from those numbers. Until Lecture 10 the signal lived on a line (time). Lecture 11 moves the same ideas onto the surface of a sphere, because the sky is a sphere around us.

Astro idea: why maps of the sky?

Many astrophysical data are a value for each direction in the sky: the temperature of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB), all-sky radio, X-ray or gamma-ray surveys, galaxy counts. Each direction is a point on a unit sphere, given by two angles: colatitude \(\theta\in[0,\pi]\) (0 at the north pole) and longitude \(\varphi\in[0,2\pi)\).

A1

Why the sphere is hard: there is no perfect grid

On a flat sheet you can draw squares of equal size, and split each square into 4 smaller squares forever. On a sphere you cannot. The obvious grid, equal steps in latitude and longitude (like a world map), crowds the poles: the cells near the pole become tiny slivers.

The reason is curvature. On a sphere, the angles of a polygon add up to more than on a plane, and the extra angle equals the area (on a unit sphere). So the shape of a regular cell fixes its size: you cannot shrink it and keep its shape. Only the 5 Platonic solids tile the sphere with identical regular cells; the finest has 20 faces (icosahedron), each about 41° across, which is useless as a map.

Every refinable grid therefore breaks something. Euler's formula \(V-E+F=2\) forces defects: a sphere covered with hexagons always needs exactly 12 pentagons (look at a football). So every pixelisation has to choose what to keep: equal areas, equal shapes, or points aligned on rings of constant latitude.

Worked numbers: polar crowding

On a latitude–longitude grid, a cell's area is proportional to \(\sin\theta\).

  • At the equator \(\theta=90^\circ\): \(\sin 90^\circ = 1\).
  • At \(\theta=10^\circ\) from the pole: \(\sin 10^\circ = 0.17\). The cell is about 6 times smaller than at the equator.
  • At \(\theta=2^\circ\): \(\sin 2^\circ = 0.035\), about 29 times smaller.

So you spend many samples near the poles where they add little information. The appendix measured an area ratio of 25 between the biggest and smallest cells of an equiangular grid of 3200 points.

A2

From the circle to the sphere

Start with a circle (one angle \(\varphi\)). Any smooth function on it is a Fourier series:

\[ f(\varphi)=\sum_k \hat f_k\, e^{ik\varphi}. \]

It is band-limited if \(\hat f_k=0\) for \(|k|>B\). The sampling theorem on the circle says: \(N\geq 2B+1\) equally spaced samples \(\varphi_n=2\pi n/N\) determine \(f\) exactly, and the DFT gives back the coefficients exactly:

\[ \hat f_k=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(\varphi_n)\,e^{-ik\varphi_n}. \]

That is the Nyquist theorem on a circle. On a torus (a doughnut, two circles) the grid is a product of two 1D grids and the 2D DFT is exact too. The sphere is not two circles: the \(\varphi\)-circles shrink to points at the poles, so the number of samples you need on each ring changes with latitude.

Worked numbers: Nyquist on a circle

Take \(f(\varphi)=\cos 3\varphi\). Its highest mode is \(k=3\), so \(B=3\). You need \(N\geq 2\cdot3+1=7\) samples, spaced \(360^\circ/7\approx 51.4^\circ\). With 7 samples the DFT returns \(\hat f_{\pm3}=0.5\) and zero for all other \(k\), to machine precision. With only 5 samples, the mode \(k=3\) looks identical to \(k=3-5=-2\): that is aliasing.

A3

Spherical harmonics: the "Fourier modes" of the sphere

On the sphere the role of \(e^{ik\varphi}\) is played by the spherical harmonics \(Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\). They are the natural vibration patterns of a sphere (eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on the sphere, \(\nabla^2_{S^2}Y_{\ell m}=-\ell(\ell+1)Y_{\ell m}\)).

\[ Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)=\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}\;P_\ell^m(\cos\theta)}_{\lambda_{\ell m}(\theta)\ \text{depends on }\theta\text{ only}}\;\; e^{im\varphi} \]
  • \(\ell\geq 0\) is the multipole: it sets the size of the pattern. Angular scale \(\theta\simeq\pi/\ell\), i.e. \(\approx 180^\circ/\ell\).
  • \(m\) runs from \(-\ell\) to \(+\ell\): \(2\ell+1\) patterns for each \(\ell\). It sets how the pattern is oriented in longitude.
  • \(m=0\): zonal, bands of latitude. \(|m|=\ell\): sectoral, segments like orange slices. In between: tesseral, a checkerboard.

Synthesis builds a map from coefficients; analysis gets the coefficients from a map:

\[ f(\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi),\qquad a_{\ell m}=\int_{S^2}f\,Y^*_{\ell m}\,d\Omega,\quad d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi. \]
Key structure to remember for the oral

\(Y_{\ell m}\) is a function of \(\theta\) times a pure Fourier wave \(e^{im\varphi}\). Along any ring of constant latitude, a spherical harmonic is just \(e^{im\varphi}\). So an FFT is hidden inside the spherical transform. This is what makes HEALPix fast (A7).

Worked numbers: counting and scales
  • Band limit \(\ell_{\max}=2\): \(\ell=0\) gives 1 coefficient, \(\ell=1\) gives 3, \(\ell=2\) gives 5. Total \(1+3+5=9=L^2\) with \(L=\ell_{\max}+1=3\).
  • \(\ell_{\max}=2048\): \(L^2\approx 4.2\) million numbers describe the map.
  • \(\ell=2\) (quadrupole): scale \(180^\circ/2=90^\circ\), patterns as big as a quarter of the sky.
  • \(\ell=220\): \(180^\circ/220\approx 0.8^\circ\). This is the first acoustic peak of the CMB, spots about one degree across (twice the Moon's diameter).
A4

The angular power spectrum \(C_\ell\) and cosmic variance

For a random sky that looks the same in all directions (statistically isotropic, like the CMB), all the information is in how much power each multipole has:

\[ \langle a_{\ell m}a^*_{\ell' m'}\rangle=C_\ell\,\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'}. \]

In words: coefficients are uncorrelated, and the average of \(|a_{\ell m}|^2\) depends only on \(\ell\). We estimate it from our one sky by averaging over the \(2\ell+1\) values of \(m\):

\[ \hat C_\ell=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}|a_{\ell m}|^2. \]

That average uses only \(2\ell+1\) numbers, so it has an unavoidable scatter. For Gaussian \(a_{\ell m}\) on the full sky:

\[ \frac{(2\ell+1)\hat C_\ell}{C_\ell}\sim\chi^2_{2\ell+1},\qquad \frac{\Delta\hat C_\ell}{C_\ell}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{2\ell+1}}. \]

This is cosmic variance. On a time series you can reduce the scatter by averaging more segments (Welch, Lecture 10). On the sky you cannot: we have only one universe to observe.

Worked numbers: estimating \(\hat C_1\) by hand

Suppose a map has \(a_{1,0}=2\), \(a_{1,1}=1+i\), \(a_{1,-1}=-(1-i)\) (for a real map \(a_{\ell,-m}=(-1)^m a^*_{\ell m}\)).

  • \(|a_{1,0}|^2=4\), \(|a_{1,1}|^2=1^2+1^2=2\), \(|a_{1,-1}|^2=2\).
  • \(\hat C_1=(4+2+2)/3=8/3\approx 2.67\).

Cosmic variance: at \(\ell=2\), \(\sqrt{2/5}=0.63\): a 63% uncertainty from the sky itself. At \(\ell=1000\), \(\sqrt{2/2001}=0.032\): 3%. Large scales are always poorly known.

Why \(2\ell+1\) degrees of freedom

\(a_{\ell 0}\) is real (1 number). For \(m>0\), \(a_{\ell m}\) is complex (2 numbers) and \(a_{\ell,-m}\) is fixed by it. So: \(1+2\ell\) independent real numbers.

A5

Discretising the transform: synthesis is exact, analysis is not

On a grid of \(N\) points \(\hat n_p\) with weights \(w_p\):

\[ \underbrace{f_p=\sum_{\ell m}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(\hat n_p)}_{\text{synthesis }S\text{: just evaluate a sum, exact}}\qquad \underbrace{\hat a_{\ell m}=\sum_p w_p f_p Y^*_{\ell m}(\hat n_p)}_{\text{analysis }A\text{: approximates an integral}} \]

The round trip \(a\to\) map \(\to\hat a\) gives back the input (\(AS=I\)) only if the sum is an exact quadrature for every product \(Y_{\ell m}Y^*_{\ell'm'}\) up to band limit \(2\ell_{\max}\). On a circle the equispaced grid does this for free. On the sphere only special grids do:

For observational data we still prefer equal-area pixels with a fast approximate transform. Detectors average over an area (they make pixels, not point samples), noise per pixel is simpler with equal areas, and we want a hierarchy to change resolution.

Worked numbers: how many samples for \(L=100\)?

Driscoll–Healy: \(4\times100^2=40\,000\). Gauss–Legendre: \(100\times(2\cdot100-1)=19\,900\). HEALPix at the same band limit: \(\approx 1.33\times 10^4=13\,300\). Fewer samples, but the round trip is no longer exact.

A6

HEALPix: Hierarchical Equal Area isoLatitude Pixelisation

HEALPix (Górski et al. 2005) is the standard for WMAP, Planck and most all-sky surveys. It keeps three properties at once:

  1. Equal area. Every pixel covers \(\Omega_{\rm pix}=4\pi/N_{\rm pix}\). Integrals become plain sums, and uniform white noise has the same variance in every pixel.
  2. Hierarchy. Each pixel splits into 4 children. Good for changing resolution and finding neighbours.
  3. Iso-latitude. Pixel centres sit on rings of constant \(\theta\), equally spaced in \(\varphi\). One FFT per ring (A7).

What it gives up: an exact quadrature (the round trip is not the identity) and uniform shapes (pixels are near-squares in some places and stretched rhombi in others).

Formulas to memorise
\[ N_{\rm pix}=12\,N_{\rm side}^2,\qquad N_{\rm side}=2^k,\qquad \Omega_{\rm pix}=\frac{\pi}{3N_{\rm side}^2},\qquad \theta_{\rm pix}=\sqrt{\Omega_{\rm pix}}\simeq\frac{58.6^\circ}{N_{\rm side}} \]

Number of rings: \(4N_{\rm side}-1\). Start: 12 base pixels (4 around each pole, 4 on the equator), each split into \(N_{\rm side}\times N_{\rm side}\).

Worked numbers: HEALPix by hand
  • \(N_{\rm side}=1\): 12 pixels, \(58.6^\circ\) each. \(N_{\rm side}=2\): 48 pixels.
  • \(N_{\rm side}=1024\) (the Lab 2 maps): \(12\times1024^2=12\,582\,912\) pixels. \(\theta_{\rm pix}=58.6^\circ/1024=0.0572^\circ=3.44'\) (arcminutes).
  • \(N_{\rm side}=2\) has \(4\cdot2-1=7\) rings. Polar cap ring \(i=1\) has \(4i=4\) pixels at \(z=\cos\theta=1-\frac{i^2}{3N_{\rm side}^2}=1-\frac{1}{12}=0.917\), so \(\theta=23.6^\circ\). The equatorial rings \(i=2\ldots6\) have \(4N_{\rm side}=8\) pixels each. Check: \(2\times4+5\times8=48\). ✓

RING and NESTED ordering

The same pixels can be numbered in two ways. RING: ring by ring from north to south; each ring is a contiguous block, which is what the transform wants. NESTED: a quad-tree inside each base pixel; the children of pixel \(p\) are \(4p,4p+1,4p+2,4p+3\). Good for neighbours and changing resolution. Planck archive files are NESTED; the lab files were converted to RING.

Worked numbers: NESTED children

Pixel 5 at \(N_{\rm side}=1\) has children 20, 21, 22, 23 at \(N_{\rm side}=2\). Their children are 80–95 at \(N_{\rm side}=4\). Upgrading or degrading a NESTED map is just regrouping blocks of 4.

A7

The fast spherical harmonic transform on rings

Because \(Y_{\ell m}=\lambda_{\ell m}(\theta)\,e^{im\varphi}\), and pixel \(j\) of ring \(r\) is at \(\varphi_{rj}=\varphi_{r0}+2\pi j/n_r\), the analysis splits into two steps:

\[ \text{1. FFT on each ring:}\quad F_m(r)=e^{-im\varphi_{r0}}\sum_{j=0}^{n_r-1}f_{rj}\,e^{-2\pi i\,mj/n_r} \] \[ \text{2. Legendre sum over rings:}\quad \hat a_{\ell m}=\Omega_{\rm pix}\sum_r\lambda_{\ell m}(\theta_r)\,F_m(r) \]

Synthesis is the same two steps in reverse. Rings are independent, so the work is trivially parallel.

Worked numbers: why this saves so much time

Direct sum: every pixel times every \((\ell,m)\): \(N_{\rm pix}\times L^2\). With \(N_{\rm pix}\approx 1.33L^2\) that is \(\sim L^4\).
Ring algorithm: \(N_{\rm ring}\times L^2\sim L^3\).
For \(L=2048\): \(L^4\approx1.8\times10^{13}\) versus \(L^3\approx8.6\times10^{9}\), about 2000 times less work. Measured with healpy on one core: \(N_{\rm side}=1024,\ \ell_{\max}=2048\) takes about 2 s. Doubling \(\ell_{\max}\) costs 6–7 times more (slope 2.6, heading to 3).

Aliasing in azimuth

A ring of \(n_r\) pixels cannot tell \(m\) from \(m\pm n_r\). The first polar ring has only 4 pixels, so \(e^{5i\varphi}\) looks like \(e^{i\varphi}\) there. This is one reason the HEALPix round trip is not exact.

How accurate is the round trip, and how to improve it

Measured at \(N_{\rm side}=64\): with \(\ell_{\max}=2N_{\rm side}\) the plain quadrature has rms error \(2\times10^{-3}\). The best \(a_{\ell m}\) solves a least-squares problem, and since \(AS\simeq I\) a simple Jacobi iteration converges fast:

\[ a^{(k+1)}=a^{(k)}+A\big(f-Sa^{(k)}\big). \]

Error: \(2.2\times10^{-3}\) (iter=0), \(2.3\times10^{-4}\) (1), \(3.3\times10^{-6}\) (3, healpy's default map2alm(iter=3)). At \(\ell_{\max}=3N_{\rm side}-1\) it stays at 0.15 whatever you do.

Practical rule

\(\ell_{\max}\leq 2N_{\rm side}\): accurate. \(2N_{\rm side}<\ell_{\max}\leq3N_{\rm side}-1\): quick looks only. \(\ell_{\max}>3N_{\rm side}\): not resolved by the grid.

A8

Beams and pixels: two low-pass filters

The pixel. A map pixel is the average of the sky over the pixel area, not the value at one point. Averaging is a convolution with a small top-hat, i.e. a low-pass filter. On average over the sphere it acts as

\[ a^{\rm map}_{\ell m}\simeq W_\ell\,a^{\rm sky}_{\ell m},\qquad C^{\rm map}_\ell\simeq W_\ell^2\,C^{\rm sky}_\ell, \]

with \(W_\ell\) the pixel window function (hp.pixwin). \(W_\ell\approx1\) for \(\ell\ll N_{\rm side}\); at \(N_{\rm side}=64\) it is 0.96 at \(\ell=N_{\rm side}\), 0.83 at \(2N_{\rm side}\), 0.64 at \(3N_{\rm side}\).

The beam. A telescope does not see a point: it sees a blurred spot, the beam, described by its FWHM (full width at half maximum). Smoothing a sky with a circular beam is a convolution on the sphere, and (Funk–Hecke theorem) it is diagonal in harmonic space: each \(a_{\ell m}\) is just multiplied by a number \(B_\ell\).

\[ a^{\rm out}_{\ell m}=B_\ell\,a_{\ell m},\qquad \text{Gaussian beam: } B_\ell=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\ell(\ell+1)\sigma^2\Big),\quad \sigma=\frac{\rm FWHM}{\sqrt{8\ln2}}=\frac{\rm FWHM}{2.355}. \]

This is the convolution theorem on the sphere. hp.smoothing does exactly: map2alm → multiply by \(B_\ell\) → alm2map. An asymmetric beam is not diagonal (it mixes different \(m\)).

Worked numbers: the Planck 70 GHz beam (you will use this tomorrow)
  • FWHM \(=13.08'\). In radians: \(13.08/60=0.218^\circ\), \(\times\pi/180=0.003805\) rad.
  • \(\sigma=0.003805/2.355=0.001616\) rad, so \(\sigma^2=2.61\times10^{-6}\).
  • \(\ell=515\): \(\ell(\ell+1)\sigma^2=515\cdot516\cdot2.61\times10^{-6}=0.694\). \(B_\ell^2=e^{-0.694}=0.50\). Half the power is gone.
  • \(\ell=800\) (third acoustic peak): \(B_\ell^2=e^{-1.67}=0.19\). Only 19% of the power survives.
  • \(\ell=1328\): \(B_\ell^2=0.01\). 99% gone.

Note we square: \(C_\ell\) is a power, so it is multiplied by \(B_\ell^2\).

\(B_\ell^2\), 70 GHz (13.08′) \(B_\ell^2\), 100 GHz (9.66′) \(W_\ell^2\), \(N_{\rm side}=1024\) (top-hat approx.)

Log scale. Where a curve drops below about 0.01, the map holds almost no sky signal at that \(\ell\). The narrower 100 GHz beam keeps signal to higher \(\ell\).

Aliasing on the sphere and choosing \(N_{\rm side}\) from the beam

If the sky has power at scales smaller than the pixels and you sample it at pixel centres, that power folds back onto lower \(\ell\) and biases \(\hat C_\ell\) upwards: aliasing, as in Lecture 7. The cure is to smooth before pixelising. A telescope does this automatically: the beam is the anti-aliasing filter of the sphere, provided the pixels are small enough compared with the beam.

A10

Changing the resolution of a map: ud_grade

Degrading (going to a smaller \(N_{\rm side}\)) with hp.ud_grade makes each new pixel the mean of its children. That mean is a top-hat of one new pixel, i.e. about 1 pixel per FWHM, which aliases badly. The fix: smooth first to a beam of about 3 new pixels, then degrade.

Upgrading copies each pixel into its 4 children. You get more pixels but no new information, and the sharp block edges add fake power at high \(\ell\).

Worked numbers: 512 → 64

Each new pixel is the mean of \((512/64)^2=64\) old pixels. Measured aliased power, directly degraded: 2.7% for \(N_{\rm side}\leq\ell<2N_{\rm side}\) and 25% for \(2N_{\rm side}\leq\ell<3N_{\rm side}\). Smoothed first to \(3\times55'=2.75^\circ\), then degraded: 0.00% and 0.03%. Upgrading back keeps only \(12\times64^2=49\,152\) independent values.

Slogan

Smooth before you degrade. Upgrading adds pixels, not information.

From the lecture appendix: smooth, then degrade
m_lo = hp.ud_grade(hp.smoothing(m, fwhm=np.radians(3*0.92*2)), 32)   # smooth, then degrade

Here \(0.92^\circ\) is the pixel size at \(N_{\rm side}=64\); the target \(N_{\rm side}=32\) has pixels twice as big, so the beam is 3 × (2 × 0.92°).

A11

Recovering the sky power spectrum (this is the whole Lab 2)

What the map gives you is the sky multiplied by the beam and the pixel window, plus noise. Solve for the sky:

\[ \hat C_\ell^{\rm map}=B_\ell^2W_\ell^2\,C_\ell^{\rm sky}+N_\ell\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat C_\ell^{\rm sky}=\frac{\hat C_\ell^{\rm map}-N_\ell}{W_\ell^2B_\ell^2}. \]
  • \(N_\ell\): the noise power spectrum (from noise simulations, from half-difference maps, or, in the lab, from the high-\(\ell\) tail).
  • \(W_\ell\) must match how the map was really made; a wrong window is a bias of several per cent at \(\ell\gtrsim N_{\rm side}\).
  • Dividing by \(W_\ell^2B_\ell^2\) is a deconvolution. Where \(B_\ell^2\) is tiny, any small error is blown up. Stop where \(B_\ell^2\) falls below the noise.
  • Error bars, full sky: \(\Delta\hat C_\ell=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{2\ell+1}}\big(C_\ell+N_\ell/(W_\ell^2B_\ell^2)\big)\): cosmic variance plus amplified noise.
Worked numbers: why deconvolution fails at high \(\ell\)

70 GHz noise: \(N=3.3\times10^{-3}\ \mu{\rm K}^2\,{\rm sr}\). At \(\ell=1500\), \(B_\ell^2=0.0028\).

  • A 1% mistake in \(N\): \(3.3\times10^{-5}\).
  • After dividing by \(B_\ell^2\): \(3.3\times10^{-5}/0.0028=0.0118\ \mu{\rm K}^2\,{\rm sr}\).
  • In \(D_\ell=\ell(\ell+1)C_\ell/2\pi\): \(1500\cdot1501\cdot0.0118/6.283\approx4\times10^3\ \mu{\rm K}^2\). The CMB itself there is under \(10^3\ \mu{\rm K}^2\). The error is bigger than the signal.
Wiener filter on the sphere (for display)

\(a^{\rm WF}_{\ell m}=\dfrac{C_\ell B_\ell W_\ell}{C_\ell B_\ell^2W_\ell^2+N_\ell}\,a^{\rm map}_{\ell m}\). The same filter as on a line (Lecture 10), now one number per \(\ell\). It gives the most probable sky for a Gaussian field: used to make pretty maps, not to measure \(C_\ell\).

A12

The cut sky: a mask is a window

Real maps are masked: we cut the Galaxy and bright sources, and keep a fraction \(f_{\rm sky}<1\) of the sphere. The mask is a window, exactly like a finite time record:

  • On a partial sky the \(Y_{\ell m}\) are no longer orthogonal: power from one \(\ell\) leaks into its neighbours. The measured "pseudo-\(C_\ell\)" is the true one blurred by a coupling matrix, \(\langle\tilde C_\ell\rangle=\sum_{\ell'}M_{\ell\ell'}C_{\ell'}\).
  • Simplest correction (used in the lab): divide by \(f_{\rm sky}\), because a masked sky keeps on average a fraction \(f_{\rm sky}\) of the power. Exact only on average and for \(\ell\) much larger than the mask's scale.
  • A patch of size \(\Theta\) cannot separate multipoles closer than \(\Delta\ell\sim2\pi/\Theta\), so we average in bins of width \(\Delta\ell\).
  • Error bars grow (Knox 1995): \(\Delta\hat C_\ell/C_\ell\simeq\sqrt{2/[(2\ell+1)f_{\rm sky}\Delta\ell]}\).
  • Sharp mask edges leak power from large scales into small ones; apodising (smoothing) the edges reduces it. Proper tools: MASTER (Hivon et al. 2002), NaMaster.

Summary and oral-exam questions

  1. The sphere has no regular grid that can be refined; HEALPix keeps equal areas, hierarchy and rings.
  2. Spherical harmonics = Legendre function × Fourier mode: transform on rings = FFT per ring + Legendre sum, \(O(L^3)\).
  3. Synthesis is exact, analysis is a quadrature: exact grids reach \(10^{-14}\); HEALPix \(2\times10^{-3}\), \(3\times10^{-6}\) after 3 iterations for \(\ell_{\max}\leq2N_{\rm side}\).
  4. The beam sets \(N_{\rm side}\): 2–3 pixels per FWHM. A 5′ beam needs \(N_{\rm side}=2048\).
  5. Smooth before you degrade. Upgrading adds pixels, not information.
  6. Recovering \(C_\ell\) is a deconvolution by \(W_\ell^2B_\ell^2\): right pixel window, a noise estimate, and a stop where the beam has killed the signal.

Test yourself (answers hidden)

1. Why can a square grid on a plane be refined forever, but a regular grid on the sphere cannot?

Show answer

On a plane the condition for a regular tiling involves only angles, so a tile can be shrunk and still fit (a square splits into 4 squares). On a sphere, by Gauss–Bonnet, the angle excess of a polygon equals its area, so shape and size are tied: a regular cell cannot shrink while keeping its shape. Only the 5 Platonic solids work, the finest with 20 cells.

2. Why is the HEALPix transform \(O(L^3)\) and not \(O(L^4)\)?

Show answer

Pixels lie on \(\sim\sqrt{N_{\rm pix}}\) rings of constant latitude, equispaced in \(\varphi\). Since \(Y_{\ell m}=\lambda_{\ell m}(\theta)e^{im\varphi}\), the \(\varphi\) part is an FFT on each ring (cheap, \(N\log N\)) and the \(\theta\) part is a Legendre sum costing \(N_{\rm ring}\times L^2\sim L^3\), instead of \(N_{\rm pix}\times L^2\sim L^4\).

3. Why does map2alm(alm2map(a)) not return exactly a?

Show answer

alm2map evaluates a finite sum (exact). map2alm approximates an integral with a sum over pixels; equal areas do not make an exact quadrature, and the short polar rings alias high \(m\). Iterating (Jacobi) reduces the error from \(2\times10^{-3}\) to \(3\times10^{-6}\) if \(\ell_{\max}\leq2N_{\rm side}\).

4. Which \(N_{\rm side}\) for a 5′ beam, and why not more?

Show answer

\(\theta_{\rm pix}=58.6^\circ/2048=1.72'\), so 5′ is 2.9 pixels per FWHM: \(N_{\rm side}=2048\). Going to 4096 would cost 4× memory and ~8× transform time for no gain, since the beam already kills the signal by \(\ell\sim1.6\)–\(2.5N_{\rm side}\).

5. What is cosmic variance, and how big is it at \(\ell=2\)?

Show answer

The scatter of \(\hat C_\ell\) because we average only \(2\ell+1\) modes of our one sky: \(\Delta C/C=\sqrt{2/(2\ell+1)}\). At \(\ell=2\): \(\sqrt{2/5}=63\%\). It cannot be reduced by better instruments.

6. What is the pixel window \(W_\ell\), and why does it appear explicitly on the sphere?

Show answer

The low-pass effect of averaging the sky over a pixel. On the sphere pixel shapes vary, so it holds only on average; and the CMB beam is measured in time-ordered data (planet transits), before map-making, so \(B_\ell\) does not contain the pixel. The pixel average is therefore a separate factor \(W_\ell^2\).

7. Why smooth before ud_grade?

Show answer

Degrading averages over one new pixel: about 1 pixel per FWHM, which lets small-scale power alias into \(\ell<3N_{\rm side}\) (25% in the example). Smoothing to ~3 new pixels first removes that power, like an anti-aliasing filter before sampling (0.03%).

8. What does a mask do to the power spectrum?

Show answer

It lowers the power by about \(f_{\rm sky}\), couples neighbouring multipoles (pseudo-\(C_\ell\), coupling matrix), lowers spectral resolution (bins of \(\Delta\ell\sim2\pi/\Theta\)), increases the variance by \(1/f_{\rm sky}\), and sharp edges leak power from low to high \(\ell\).

The project at a glance

The question

So far the skies were simulated and the beams known. On a real map, with our Galaxy in the way, instrument noise and a measured beam, how much of the true CMB power spectrum can we recover? We answer it with the Planck 70 GHz map (core) and the 100 GHz map (extensions).

The measurement equation (the math behind everything)

\[ a^{\rm obs}_{\ell m}=B_\ell W_\ell\,a^{\rm sky}_{\ell m}+n_{\ell m},\qquad \hat C_\ell=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_m|a_{\ell m}|^2,\qquad \langle\hat C_\ell\rangle=B_\ell^2W_\ell^2\,C^{\rm sky}_\ell+N_\ell \]

In words: the observed map = sky blurred by the beam (\(B_\ell\)) and by the pixels (\(W_\ell\)), plus noise. Its power spectrum = blurred sky power + a noise floor. \(C^{\rm sky}_\ell\) = CMB + Galaxy + extragalactic sources; only the CMB is in the model you compare with. We plot \(D_\ell=\ell(\ell+1)C_\ell/2\pi\) in \(\mu{\rm K}^2\), the standard way to show CMB spectra (it makes the peaks visible; a flat \(C_\ell\) becomes \(D_\ell\propto\ell^2\)).

Astrophysics concepts you need for this lab

CMB

The Cosmic Microwave Background is light released about 380 000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe became transparent. Today it is a 2.725 K glow from every direction. Its tiny temperature differences, about 100 µK (1 part in ~30 000), are the seeds of all galaxies.

Planck

ESA satellite (2009–2013) that mapped the whole sky in 9 frequency bands from 30 to 857 GHz. LFI (Low Frequency Instrument): 30, 44, 70 GHz. HFI (High Frequency Instrument): 100 to 857 GHz. Final release: 2018 (PR3).

Why two frequencies

In \(\mu{\rm K}_{\rm CMB}\) units the CMB looks identical at every frequency. The Galaxy does not: synchrotron and free-free emission (bright at low frequency) and dust (bright at high frequency). Around 70–100 GHz the Galaxy is faintest. Comparing channels separates sky components.

Galactic coordinates, Mollweide

Planck maps use Galactic longitude \(l\) and latitude \(b\): the map's equator is the plane of the Milky Way, the centre is towards the Galactic centre. The Mollweide projection draws the whole sphere as an ellipse with equal areas.

Monopole and dipole

\(\ell=0\) (monopole) is the sky average: the 2.725 K is removed. \(\ell=1\) (dipole) is a hot/cold pair of halves caused by our motion (~370 km/s) relative to the CMB: about 3 mK, also removed by Planck. What remains in the map at \(\ell=0,1\) comes from the Galaxy and offsets.

Acoustic peaks

Before the CMB was released, the hot plasma rang with sound waves. They imprint peaks in \(D_\ell\): the first at \(\ell\approx220\) (≈1°), then near 540 and 810. Their positions and heights measure the geometry, matter and dark-matter content of the universe.

Beam and FWHM

A telescope's resolution: a point source looks like a blob of width FWHM. 70 GHz: 13.08′; 100 GHz: 9.66′. 1′ (arcminute) = 1/60 of a degree. The beam removes power at high \(\ell\) by \(B_\ell^2\).

Noise and foregrounds

Detector noise is random in each pixel ("white"): its spectrum is flat, \(N_\ell\) = constant, and it is not blurred by the beam. Foregrounds are real sky signals that are not CMB: Galaxy, and distant radio galaxies too faint to mask (point sources).

Units: µK² sr

\(C_\ell\) has units of temperature² × solid angle (steradians). \(D_\ell=\ell(\ell+1)C_\ell/2\pi\) is in \(\mu{\rm K}^2\). The model peak is \(D_{220}\approx5730\ \mu{\rm K}^2\), i.e. \(C_{220}=5730\cdot2\pi/(220\cdot221)=0.74\ \mu{\rm K}^2\,{\rm sr}\).

What is left after the correction?

Goal: at 70 GHz the recovered spectrum exceeds the model more and more with \(\ell\) (1.05, 1.15, 1.42). Find the shape of the excess and fit it.

Mini math: weighted least squares for one number

Model: \(y_\ell=A\,x_\ell\) with \(y=\hat C_\ell-N-C^{\rm th}_\ell B_\ell^2W_\ell^2\) (the excess) and \(x=B_\ell^2W_\ell^2\) (its shape if it is a flat \(C_\ell\) seen through the beam). Minimising \(\chi^2=\sum(y-Ax)^2/\sigma^2\) gives

\[ A=\frac{\sum x y/\sigma^2}{\sum x^2/\sigma^2}. \]

Toy check: \(x=[1,0.5]\), \(y=[2,1]\), \(\sigma^2=[1,1]\): \(A=(2+0.5)/(1+0.25)=2\). Exactly the slope. Then compare \(\chi^2\) with \(A\) and with \(A=0\): a big drop means the fit explains the excess.

\[ \hat C^{70\times100}_\ell=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_m a^{70}_{\ell m}a^{100\,*}_{\ell m},\qquad \langle\hat C^{70\times100}_\ell\rangle=B^{70}_\ell B^{100}_\ell W_\ell^2\,C^{\rm sky}_\ell\quad(\text{no }N_\ell). \]
Mini math

Each map = sky + its own noise: \(a^{70}=s+n_1\), \(a^{100}=s+n_2\). Product: \(s^2+s\,n_2+n_1 s+n_1n_2\). The noises are uncorrelated with the sky and with each other, so the last three terms average to 0. Only \(s^2\) survives: nothing to subtract. Beams: one factor of each, \(B^{70}B^{100}\), instead of a square.

Questions

Compare cross, auto 70 and auto 100 (all corrected): what improves?

Show answer

There is no noise to subtract, so no error from estimating it. At \(\ell\simeq1000\) the cross gives 1.59 times the model, the auto 70 gives 1.96.

Is the cross-spectrum the CMB alone?

Show answer

No. Everything on the sky is in both maps, including Galaxy residuals and point sources: cross/model is 1.13 at \(\ell\simeq800\). The cross removes the noise bias, not the foregrounds. Real analyses cross two halves of the same channel (half-mission, detector sets).
