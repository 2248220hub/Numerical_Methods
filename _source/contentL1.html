This laboratory puts Lectures 7–10 to work on real files: from WAV samples to spectra, from spectra to filters, and back to sound.

The analysis is in the notebook AstroSignal_corrected.ipynb (Python only). Each lesson of this page explains one or two blocks of it.

Every filter here is a real weight H(f) ∈ [0, 1] applied to the FFT of the whole record, with H = 0 at the 50 Hz mains hum.

OBJECTIVEWhat we want to find out

  1. Read a WAV file and inspect it in time and in frequency; say what each spectral estimate shows.
  2. Choose the window and the segment length of a spectral estimate (Welch).
  3. Check with a spectrogram whether the signal is stationary.
  4. Filter in the frequency domain — band-pass, comb, Wiener — go back to time and listen.
  5. Recognise and remove a systematic line: the 50 Hz mains hum.
  6. Compare every filtered output with the clean signal, with a number (SNR) and a plot.
  7. Fit a model to the noise, set a detection threshold, build a Wiener filter from the data alone.

BLOCKS 11–12 · WHY THE NAIVE FILTER FAILS, AND THE FIX
naive Wiener
Wiener on detected lines
clean reference

The Wiener filter keeps each frequency in proportion to how much signal it contains. With single periodogram bins, “how much signal” is itself a noisy number: 37% of noise bins look like signal.

Keep a bin only if z exceeds a threshold that noise alone reaches once in a hundred recordings: z* = ln(M/p) = 19.4.
  • Φ = Ss/(Ss+Sn) = 1 − 1/z
  • The exponential statistics of z
  • The detection threshold z*
  • Systematic lines are detected too
\h{Phi}{\Phi_k}=\frac{S_s}{S_s+\h{Sn}{S_n}}=1-\frac{1}{\h{zk}{z_k}},\qquad \h{zk}{z_k}=\frac{\h{Pk}{P_k}}{\h{Sn}{S_n(f_k)}},\qquad S_s\approx P_k-S_n All quantities in the same units (one-sided density). With P = |X|²/N instead of 2|X|²/(fsN), Φ ≈ 1 everywhere and nothing is filtered (SNR −45 dB). Pk = np.abs(X)**2 * 2 / (fs * N) z = Pk / Sn; phi = np.clip(1 - 1/z, 0, 1)

Block 11 — The naive Wiener filter

Reading the results. In a noise-only bin z is exponential: P(z > 1) = e−1 = 0.368 (measured 0.369). The naive filter keeps 976 472 bins, a third of the spectrum, including the huge low-frequency noise: SNR −37.5 dB, the output is offset by −3000 and noisy. P − Sn in a single bin is not an estimate of Ss: it fluctuates as much as the noise.

\h{M}{M}\,e^{-\h{zstar}{z^*}}=\h{pfa}{p}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \h{zstar}{z^*}=\ln\frac{\h{M}{M}}{\h{pfa}{p}}=\ln\frac{2.64\times10^6}{0.01}=19.4 M bins tested (above 20 Hz), p = expected number of false lines per recording. The largest noise-only z above 20 Hz was 15.3 — below the threshold, as it should be. zstar = np.log(np.sum(f > 20) / 0.01)

The periodogram values are independent exponentials around S(f): their exact likelihood gives the Whittle estimator, the best fit once the lines are excluded.

RESULTSConclusions

  1. Lines vs noise. A signal 45 dB below the noise stands 4–5×10⁴ above it in single periodogram bins: steady tones put all their power in one bin of 0.008 Hz.
  2. Welch shows the noise, the periodogram the lines. Averaging 160 segments reduces the scatter to 8% but the lines to 240–340; the window matters for a red spectrum (rectangular: ×2 noise at 50–100 Hz).
  3. Resolution is a choice. 440 and 442 Hz merge with 1.5 s segments and separate with 3 s; in a spectrogram they are a 2 Hz beat (0.1 s) or two lines (2 s).
  4. Knowing where the noise is pays. A band-pass by eye brings the chord back (+5.7 dB); a comb with 2.5 Hz teeth +19 dB; a low-pass would do nothing.
  5. Systematics. The 50 Hz hum is detected like a signal (z = 3544) and must be vetoed by hand.
  6. Wiener needs a good Ss. From single bins it keeps 37% of the noise; from the detected lines it returns the clean signal. The noise model (fk = 440 Hz, α = 2) is recovered by least squares on log Welch and, best, by Whittle with the lines masked.
Where it can fail: wrong units in Φ (factor 2/(fsN)); systematic lines treated as signal; Ss from a smoothed (Welch) estimate spreads the line power and lets noise in; non-stationary lines break whole-record filters (use the STFT).
