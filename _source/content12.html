The whole module (Lectures 12–14) answers one question: from one noisy detector record, what are the three numbers of its noise model, and how sure are we?

Before any formula we need a language for “how sure”. That language is probability — and there are three ways to read it.

Frequentists and Bayesians use the same maths (Kolmogorov's axioms). They disagree on what a probability can be attached to: only repeatable data, or also an unknown parameter.

BASICThe problem of the module: three numbers from one record

A detector records noise for 64 s at 100 Hz: N = 6400 samples. Its power spectrum is not flat: at low frequency there is extra power (“1/f noise”) that bends up below a knee frequency. The model used in every lecture of this module is

\h{Sf}{S(f)} = \h{s2}{\sigma^2}\left[1+\left(\frac{\h{fk}{f_k}}{\h{f}{f}}\right)^{\h{alpha}{\alpha}}\right] Three parameters θ = (σ², fk, α). The simulation's truth is (10⁻³, 1 Hz, 1). Above the knee the noise is white (flat, level σ²); below it, power grows like f−α. def S(f, sigma2, fk, alpha): return sigma2 * (1.0 + (fk / f) ** alpha) pure elemental real(dp) function S(f, sigma2, fk, alpha) real(dp), intent(in) :: f, sigma2, fk, alpha S = sigma2 * (1.0_dp + (fk/f)**alpha) end function S

Fitting one record (Maximum Likelihood, lesson 3) gives \(\hat f_k = 0.93\) Hz and \(\hat\alpha = 0.82\). The truth is 1 and 1. How far from the truth can we be, with one record? What does “\(f_k = 0.93 \pm 0.2\) Hz” actually mean? Answering that honestly is the goal of Lecture 12.

BASICTwo kinds of uncertainty

  • Epistemic — from our limited knowledge or finite instrument precision. The system may be deterministic (a tossed coin), but we don't know its initial conditions. Reducible by collecting more information. → Our ignorance of (σ², fk, α).
  • Aleatory (intrinsic) — in the phenomenon itself (a quantum measurement), or variability we choose to model as random. → The detector noise: another record, another periodogram.

Bayesian probability: the degree of belief (plausibility) in a proposition, given the information available. It applies to single events and to parameters: “\(P(0.8 \lt f_k \lt 1.2\ {\rm Hz} \mid {\rm data})\)” makes sense. It is not arbitrary: Cox (1946) showed that any consistent calculus of plausibility obeying common-sense requirements is probability theory. “Subjective” means conditional on the information, not “as you please”.

COREThe law of large numbers

The frequency nE/N does not equal p after N trials; it wanders around p with a typical distance that shrinks like 1/√N. Precisely, nE is binomial, so

\h{sdfreq}{\sigma\!\left(\tfrac{n_E}{N}\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{\h{p}{p}\,(1-\h{p}{p})}{\h{N}{N}}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P\!\left(\left|\tfrac{n_E}{N}-p\right|\gt\epsilon\right)\to 0 The law of large numbers is a theorem of the axioms (convergence in probability), not the definition. The 1/√N law is the seed of everything in Lecture 13: every Monte Carlo error bar is this formula in disguise. se = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / N) se = sqrt(p*(1.0_dp - p)/N) Two fair dice: what is the classical probability that the sum is 7? (decimal or fraction) Count the ordered pairs (a,b) with a+b = 7, out of 36. (1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1): 6 favourable cases, so P = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.1667. An event has p = 0.3. After N = 100 trials, what is the standard deviation of the relative frequency nE/N? \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\) \(\sqrt{0.3\cdot 0.7/100} = \sqrt{0.0021} = 0.0458\). So seeing 0.25 or 0.35 after 100 trials is completely normal (about 1σ). With p = 0.5, how many trials N do you need so that σ(nE/N) = 0.005? Solve \(0.005 = \sqrt{0.25/N}\). \(N = 0.25/0.005^2 = 0.25/2.5\times10^{-5} = 10\,000\). Halving the error costs four times more trials — the 1/√N law.

BASICConditional probability

We often want the probability of A knowing that B happened (with P(B) > 0). We restrict the world to B and ask which fraction of it is also A:

\h{PAB}{P(A\mid B)} = \frac{\h{cap}{P(A\cap B)}}{\h{PB}{P(B)}} Example: one die, A = “6”, B = “even”. P(A∩B) = 1/6, P(B) = 1/2, so P(6 | even) = 1/3. p_A_given_B = p_AB / p_B p_A_given_B = p_AB / p_B

Multiplying out gives the product rule, written two ways because ∩ is symmetric:

\h{cap}{P(A\cap B)} = \h{PAB}{P(A\mid B)}\,\h{PB}{P(B)} = \h{PBA}{P(B\mid A)}\,\h{PA}{P(A)}

If A₁ … An split Ω into disjoint pieces (a partition: exactly one is true), then B happens through exactly one of them:

\h{PB}{P(B)} = \h{sum}{\sum_i} \h{PBAi}{P(B\mid A_i)}\,\h{PAi}{P(A_i)} p_B = np.sum(p_B_given_A * p_A) # arrays over the partition p_B = sum(p_B_given_A * p_A)

A and B are independent if \(P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)\), i.e. \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\): knowing B teaches nothing about A.

BASICBayes' theorem in two lines

  1. Write the product rule both ways: \(P(A\mid B)P(B) = P(B\mid A)P(A)\).
  2. Divide by \(P(B)\) and expand \(P(B)\) with the total probability. Done.
\h{PAB}{P(A\mid B)} = \frac{\h{PBA}{P(B\mid A)}\;\h{PA}{P(A)}}{\h{PB}{P(B)}},\qquad \h{PB}{P(B)}=\h{sum}{\sum_i}\h{PBAi}{P(B\mid A_i)}\h{PAi}{P(A_i)} The engine of inference: it inverts the conditioning, from “effect given cause” (easy to model) to “cause given observed effect” (what we want). def bayes(like, prior): """like[i] = P(B | A_i), prior[i] = P(A_i) -> P(A_i | B)""" joint = like * prior return joint / joint.sum() # divide by the evidence P(B) post = like * prior post = post / sum(post) ! divide by the evidence P(B)

For a model with parameters θ and data x (plus background information I, usually left implicit) every piece gets a name:

\underbrace{\h{post}{p(\theta\mid x)}}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{\h{like}{p(x\mid\theta)}}^{\text{likelihood}}\ \overbrace{\h{prior}{p(\theta)}}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{\h{evid}{p(x)}}_{\text{evidence}}},\qquad \h{evid}{p(x)} = \h{integral}{\int} \h{like}{p(x\mid\theta)}\,\h{prior}{p(\theta)}\dd\theta The evidence normalises the posterior: a constant for one model (ignore it for parameter estimation), the key quantity to compare models (lesson 7, Expert). # on a grid of theta values: post = like(theta_grid) * prior(theta_grid) post /= np.trapz(post, theta_grid) # = dividing by the evidence post = like * prior post = post / (sum(post) * dtheta) ! Riemann-sum evidence
Before data you have a belief (prior). The data say how well each possible value explains what you saw (likelihood). Multiply, renormalise: that's your new belief (posterior).

BASICWorked example: the two urns (from the lecture)

Urn A₁: 3 blue, 7 red. Urn A₂: 6 blue, 4 red. One is chosen at random: P(A₁) = P(A₂) = ½. We draw a ball: blue.

  1. Likelihoods: P(B | A₁) = 0.3, P(B | A₂) = 0.6.
  2. Evidence: P(B) = 0.3·½ + 0.6·½ = 0.45.
  3. Posterior: P(A₁ | B) = 0.3·½ / 0.45 = 1/3, P(A₂ | B) = 2/3.
  4. Put the ball back, draw again: blue. Yesterday's posterior is today's prior: P(A₁ | BB) = 0.3·⅓ / (0.3·⅓ + 0.6·⅔) = 0.2.
  5. Same in one go with the likelihood of two draws: 0.3²·½ / (0.3²·½ + 0.6²·½) = 0.2. The order in which data arrive does not matter.
Same urns, equal priors. You draw one ball and it is red. What is P(A₁ | red)? P(red | A₁) = 0.7, P(red | A₂) = 0.4. Evidence 0.7·½ + 0.4·½ = 0.55. P(A₁ | R) = 0.35/0.55 = 7/11 ≈ 0.636. Equal priors. Two draws with replacement: blue then red. What is P(A₁ | BR)? Likelihood of the pair: P(B|A)·P(R|A). The equal priors cancel. A₁: 0.3·0.7 = 0.21; A₂: 0.6·0.4 = 0.24. P(A₁|BR) = 0.21/(0.21+0.24) = 0.467. One blue and one red almost cancel out — but not exactly, because the two urns are not symmetric.

COREThe base-rate trap: why priors matter

A transient-detection pipeline (supernovae, GW alerts, a satellite fault monitor) raises an alarm. It catches 99% of real events (sensitivity) and has a 5% false-alarm rate. Real events are rare: 1% of candidates. Given an alarm, what is the probability it's real? Most people say “99%”. Bayes says:

\h{PRA}{P(\text{real}\mid\text{alarm})} = \frac{\h{sens}{P(\text{alarm}\mid\text{real})}\;\h{prev}{P(\text{real})}}{\h{sens}{P(\text{alarm}\mid\text{real})}\h{prev}{P(\text{real})} + \h{fa}{P(\text{alarm}\mid\text{noise})}\,[1-\h{prev}{P(\text{real})}]} With 0.99, 0.01, 0.05: 0.0099 / (0.0099 + 0.0495) = 0.167. Five out of six alarms are false, because noise candidates are 99 times more numerous. post = sens * prev / (sens * prev + fa * (1 - prev)) post = sens*prev / (sens*prev + fa*(1.0_dp - prev))
Compute P(real | alarm) for prevalence 1%, sensitivity 99%, false-alarm rate 5%. Numerator 0.99·0.01; denominator adds 0.05·0.99. 0.0099/(0.0099+0.0495) = 0.0099/0.0594 = 0.1667. Two independent alarms on the same candidate (both fire). Use the posterior of the first (0.1667) as the prior of the second. New P(real)? Repeat the formula with prev = 1/6. 0.99·(1/6) / (0.99/6 + 0.05·5/6) = 0.165/(0.165+0.04167) = 0.798. Sequential updating: a second, independent test is very informative.

COREThe likelihood of a spectrum: Whittle

Our noise samples are correlated (that's what a non-white spectrum means), so the time-domain likelihood is not a product of independent Gaussians. Go to the frequency domain: for a stationary Gaussian process, the periodogram bins Pj (DC and Nyquist excluded) are asymptotically independent and exponential with mean S(fj), i.e. Pj = S(fj) χ²₂/2.

Mean and standard deviation of Pj are both Sj: a periodogram is 100% noisy per bin. BASICSharp peak = precise parameter

Near its minimum, −ln 𝓛 looks like a parabola. A narrow parabola means a small change of θ makes the data much less probable: θ is well constrained. The curvature (second derivative) measures this.

\h{F}{F_{ab}} = \h{E}{\mathbb E}\!\left[-\frac{\partial^2 \h{lnL}{\ln\mathcal L}}{\partial\h{theta}{\theta_a}\,\partial\h{theta}{\theta_b}}\right],\qquad\text{Whittle: }\ \h{F}{F_{ab}}=\h{sum}{\sum_j}\partial_a\ln \h{Sj}{S_j}\;\partial_b\ln \h{Sj}{S_j} The expectation is over data sets. Derivation for the simplest case: the mean of N Gaussians

  1. \(-\ln\mathcal L(\mu) = \sum_i (x_i-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2) + {\rm const}\).
  2. Second derivative: \(\partial^2(-\ln\mathcal L)/\partial\mu^2 = N/\sigma^2\) (no data left: expectation is trivial).
  3. So \(F = N/\sigma^2\) and the error is \(\sigma_\mu = 1/\sqrt F = \sigma/\sqrt N\). The famous “standard error of the mean” is a Fisher error.

COREMLE versus MAP

\h{thetamap}{\hat\theta_{\rm MAP}} = \h{argmax}{\arg\max_\theta}\ \big[\h{L}{\mathcal L(\theta)}\,\h{pi}{\pi(\theta)}\big] Includes the prior; penalises values that fit but are a-priori improbable. Not invariant: a density changes with the Jacobian, so its maximum moves. Same record, marginal of fk: MAP 0.87 Hz (flat in log fk) vs 0.92 Hz (flat in fk). Quantiles (the median) are invariant under monotonic maps; MAP and mean are not. theta_map = minimize(lambda t: -(log_like(t) + log_prior(t)), x0).x imap = maxloc(lnL + ln_prior, dim=1)

When do they coincide? (1) Flat prior in the chosen parametrisation: \(\arg\max[\mathcal L\cdot k] = \hat\theta_{\rm ML}\) — the same number, not the same meaning. (2) Large N: ln 𝓛 is a sum of N terms, its curvature grows like N, the posterior width shrinks like 1/√N while the prior stays fixed; within that width a smooth prior is nearly constant. The posterior → Gaussian with covariance F⁻¹ (Bernstein–von Mises). Conditions: prior non-zero at the truth, identifiable model, fixed number of parameters.

COREOne posterior, several “best values”
