A hyperspectral image is a cube: two spatial axes and 100–200 wavelength bands. Each pixel is a full spectrum, a fingerprint of the materials on the ground.

This page collects the project parts of every lesson. Each part starts with a link back to the theory.

Start on synthetic data where the truth is known (abundances, noise per band), check that every method recovers it, then move to real scenes.

Each pixel spectrum y (120 bands) is modelled as E θ: four endmember spectra mixed with fractions θ. 16 384 small least-squares problems — or one big one if neighbouring pixels are tied together.

Above: the singular values of the whitened cube, and how they change with the noise and the number of materials.

Weight the bands by their noise first (divide by σb), then: Cholesky or QR per pixel; SVD to see how many independent spectra there are; CG for the coupled, regularised problem.

The SVD counts the materials

Centre the whitened cube (16 384 × 120) and take its SVD (0.25 s). With unit-variance noise, pure noise gives singular values ≈ √Npix = 128. Measured: 16 656, 10 301, 5 136, then 138, 138, 138, … — three components above the noise plus the mean: four materials. A rank-3 reconstruction is 6 times closer to the noiseless cube than the data (0.16 σ vs 1 σ): the SVD is a denoiser that knows nothing about the endmembers.

\min_{\Theta}\ \sum_p\|W(\mathbf y_p-E\boldsymbol\theta_p)\|^2+\lambda\sum_{p\sim q}\|\boldsymbol\theta_p-\boldsymbol\theta_q\|^2\ \Rightarrow\ \big(I_N\otimes G+\lambda\,L_{\rm grid}\otimes I_4\big)\,\mathrm{vec}\,\Theta=\mathrm{vec}(E^TW^2Y) 4 × 128 × 128 = 65 536 unknowns, a sparse SPD matrix never built: one product costs a 4×4 multiply per pixel plus a 5-point Laplacian per material. Everything in Lesson 7 happens along the wavelength axis too: the spectrum is a continuous signal, the band centres are the samples, the band response is the anti-alias filter.

If the bands are narrower than their spacing (FWHM < Δλ), features narrower than Δλ are recorded or missed depending on where the band grid falls: spectral aliasing.

Lesson 7.3 · Sampling, Nyquist and aliasing y_j=\frac{\int \h{srf}{R_j(\lambda)}\,\rho(\lambda)\,d\lambda}{\int R_j(\lambda)\,d\lambda},\qquad R_j(\lambda)=\exp\!\Big[-\frac{(\lambda-\lambda_0-j\,\h{dlam}{\Delta\lambda})^2}{2s^2}\Big],\quad s=\frac{\h{fwhm}{\rm FWHM}}{2.355} The spectrum ρ(λ) is convolved with the response R and sampled every Δλ. A Gaussian SRF with FWHM ≳ Δλ suppresses detail finer than the sampling — the role of the anti-alias filter of Lesson 7. W = np.exp(-0.5*((lam[None, :] - centres[:, None]) / s)**2) bands = (W * spectrum).sum(1) / W.sum(1) do j = 1, nb w = exp(-0.5_dp*((lam - centres(j))/s)**2) bands(j) = sum(w*spectrum)/sum(w) end do

Quantisation: rounding reflectance in [0, 1] to B bits adds Δ/√12 = 2−B/√12. With 6 bits (4.5×10⁻³) this is as large as the sensor noise (4×10⁻³); with 8 bits it is 1.1×10⁻³, negligible. Real sensors digitise radiance with 12–16 bits because bright snow and dark water must fit in the same range.

A push-broom imager (PRISMA, EnMAP, AVIRIS-NG) has a line of detectors across the track and builds the image row by row as the platform moves. A detector a bit brighter than its neighbours draws a stripe down the whole image.

A pattern that depends on x only has its 2D spectrum on the v = 0 axis. Removing that line (except the lowest |u|) is the column-mean destriper written in Fourier space.

Lesson 13.2 · The sample mean and its error Lesson 13.6 · Self-normalised importance sampling
  1. Sample mean: the mean spectrum of a class from N random pixels, ±σ/√N per band; rms error ∝ N−0.49 (Monte Carlo: −0.5).
  2. Hit-or-miss: fraction of the scene with NDVI > 0.4 (truth 0.382): N = 300 → 0.387, Wilson 95% [0.333, 0.443]; N = 3000 → 0.394, [0.377, 0.412].
  3. Error propagation by simulation: NDVI of one pixel, 0.8666 ± 0.0141 by Monte Carlo and by the linear formula — the noise is small; make it larger and the linear formula fails.
  4. Self-normalised importance sampling: abundances with the physical prior (θk ≥ 0, Σθ ≈ 1), Gaussian posterior as proposal. Neff/N = 0.64 (×1), 0.26 (×1.5), 0.023 (×3): a wider proposal is not always better in 4 dimensions.
\mathrm{NDVI}=\frac{\h{NIR}{N}-\h{Rb}{R}}{\h{NIR}{N}+\h{Rb}{R}},\qquad \sigma_{\rm NDVI}^2\approx\Big(\frac{2N}{(N+R)^2}\Big)^2\sigma_R^2+\Big(\frac{2R}{(N+R)^2}\Big)^2\sigma_N^2 The Monte Carlo way needs no derivatives: simulate R and N with their noise, compute NDVI for each, take the histogram. r_s = R + sR * rng.standard_normal(100_000) n_s = NIR + sN * rng.standard_normal(100_000) ndvi = (n_s - r_s) / (n_s + r_s) print(ndvi.mean(), ndvi.std()) call normal_deviates(zr); call normal_deviates(zn) ndvi = ((NIR + sN*zn) - (R + sR*zr)) / ((NIR + sN*zn) + (R + sR*zr))
